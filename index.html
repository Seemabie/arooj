What to expect

Two rounds, different focus each. Here is the honest breakdown so nothing surprises you.

Round 1 · May 7 · Recruiter Screen

This one is a conversation more than a test. The recruiter wants to confirm the basics and get a feel for whether you would actually thrive in this role.

Length
30 to 45 minutes, usually a phone or video call.
Focus
Your background, why Amazon, why this role, willingness to travel up to 90 percent, physical comfort with the work, salary expectations.
Win it by
Sounding genuine about why this role fits you. Show you have read the job description and you understand what 90 percent travel actually means in your life.
Watch for
Soft Leadership Principle questions tucked inside friendly chat. Even here, Amazon is listening.

Round 2 · May 12 · Loop Interview

This is the deeper round. Expect a mix of behavioral questions tied to the Leadership Principles and technical questions about the actual work.

Length
Often 45 to 60 minutes per interviewer, sometimes back to back.
Focus
Behavioral questions in STAR format, hands on technical questions about cabling, fiber, copper, racks, safety, and troubleshooting.
Win it by
Having three to five strong stories prepared in STAR form. Each story should map to two or three Leadership Principles.
Watch for
Bar Raiser style follow ups. They will dig deeper after your answer with "why did you choose that approach", "what would you do differently", "what was the result in numbers".
★ The Amazon truth
  • Every Amazon interview is graded against the 16 Leadership Principles, even the technical ones.
  • Each interviewer is assigned 2 to 3 specific principles to evaluate. They submit independent feedback.
  • Use "I" not "we". They are hiring you, not your team.
  • Quantify your results. "I reduced rework by 30 percent" lands harder than "it went well".
  • Prepare for follow up questions. They will probe 3 to 4 layers deep.

Answer Framework

The STAR Method

Every behavioral answer follows this structure. Memorize the four pieces and you will never get lost mid story.

Step One

Situation

Set the scene in 2 to 3 sentences. Where were you, what was happening, why did it matter.

"At my current role at AWS, our team was deploying a new switch rack on a tight deadline. The original cable plan had the wrong fiber type listed for two uplinks."
Step Two

Task

Make the goal or your responsibility crystal clear in 1 to 2 sentences. What were you specifically supposed to do.

"As the lead installer on shift, I had to fix the plan and complete the install before the customer cutover window closed in three hours."
Step Three

Action

The longest part. Describe what YOU did, step by step. Use "I" not "we". Include 2 to 4 specific actions.

"I checked the spec sheet on the SFP modules, confirmed they needed OS2 single mode, pulled the right cables from stock, re-labeled them, ran them through the cable management with proper bend radius, and tested each run with an optical power meter before patching."
Step Four

Result

End with measurable impact. Numbers, time saved, problem prevented, customer outcome. This is what they remember.

"We finished the install 25 minutes inside the window with zero failed test runs. The customer cutover went clean, and I added a fiber type checklist to our pre-install template so the same error would not happen again."
★ STAR mistakes to avoid
  • Saying "we" instead of "I" in the action part. The interviewer is grading you, not the team.
  • Spending too long on the situation. Aim for 30 percent of your time on Action and Result.
  • Vague results like "it went well". Always have a number, a timeframe, or a specific outcome.
  • Not preparing follow ups. After your answer, they will ask "why that approach" or "what would you do differently".
  • Using a story that is older than 3 to 4 years if you have something more recent.

Build your story bank

Pick 5 to 7 strong stories from your work life. Each story should be flexible enough to answer questions about 2 or 3 different Leadership Principles. That way you walk in with material you can adapt.

A time you solved a problem A time you took ownership A time you helped a teammate A safety call you made A mistake and recovery A time you learned fast A time you finished early

Hands On Knowledge

Technical cheat sheet

Everything you might be asked about cabling, hardware, and safety. Speak naturally about these and you will sound like the pro you are.

Fiber Optic Cables

Two big families, picked based on distance and bandwidth needs.

Single mode
Small core (around 9 microns), used for long distance and high bandwidth. Color jacket is yellow. Standard is OS2.
Multi mode
Larger core (50 microns), used for short distances inside data centers. Common types are OM3 (aqua), OM4 (aqua or magenta), OM5 (lime green).
Connectors
LC is most common (small, push pull). MPO/MTP is used for high-density 40G/100G/400G. SC is older, larger.

Copper Cables

Twisted pair Ethernet, the workhorse for shorter runs.

Cat5e
Up to 1 Gbps, 100 meters max. Older but still common.
Cat6
Up to 10 Gbps over 55 meters, 1 Gbps to 100 meters.
Cat6a
10 Gbps to full 100 meters. Standard for new data center deployments.
Wiring standards
T568A and T568B define the pin colors. Most data centers use T568B. Both ends must match for straight through.

Cable Management Best Practices

Bend radius
Never bend fiber tighter than 10 times the cable diameter. Around 30mm minimum for most patch fiber.
Velcro vs zip ties
Use Velcro. Zip ties can crush cables and damage them when removed. Velcro is reusable and gentle.
Labeling
Label both ends with source, destination, and port. Update DCIM or your cable spreadsheet immediately.
Slack
Leave a service loop near each end so you can rework without tension on the connector.

Rack Basics

1U
One rack unit equals 1.75 inches. Used to measure equipment height in a rack.
Standard rack
42U is the most common full size. Width is 19 inches.
Rails
Inner rails attach to the server, outer rails attach to the rack posts. Make sure they are level.
Heavy gear
Use a server lift or do a 2-person lift for anything over 40 pounds. Never wing it.

Power and PDUs

PDU
Power Distribution Unit. Vertical strips on the side of the rack delivering power to gear.
Redundancy
Always plug the two power supplies of a server into separate PDUs. That is the whole point of dual PSU.
A side and B side
Two independent power paths. If one fails, the other carries the load.

Airflow and Cooling

Hot aisle / cold aisle
Cold aisles in front (where servers pull air in), hot aisles behind (where they push exhaust out). Never mix them.
Blanking panels
Fill empty rack U slots with blanking panels so cold air does not bypass the gear.
Cable obstruction
Messy cables in the back can block exhaust airflow and cause overheating. Keep them tight.

Test Equipment

Cable tester
Checks copper for continuity, shorts, miswires. Fluke is the industry name.
Optical power meter
Measures the light coming out of a fiber. You compare to expected dB to know if the run is healthy.
Light source
Pairs with the power meter. Sends a known signal so you can measure end-to-end loss.
OTDR
Optical Time Domain Reflectometer. Tells you exactly where in a fiber a break or bad splice is located.
VFL
Visual Fault Locator. Red laser that lights up the fiber so you can spot breaks visually.

Hand Tools

Crimper Punch down tool Cable cutter Stripper Multi tool Torque screwdriver ESD strap Headlamp

A torque screwdriver matters because over-tightening rack screws can strip threads and crack equipment ears. Always set the right torque value.

Diagnosing a Bad Cable

The order matters. Start cheap, work up to expensive.

1. Visual
Look at the connector for damage, dirt, or bent pins. Most fiber issues are dirty connectors.
2. Re seat
Unplug, inspect, plug back in. Many "cable issues" are loose connectors.
3. Tester
Run a continuity test for copper, or a power meter test for fiber.
4. Swap
Replace with a known good cable. If it works, you confirmed the cable was the issue.
5. Replace and document
Pull the bad cable, label it as failed, log it. Never leave failed cables in service.

Lifting Rules

Up to 40 lbs
Solo lift is acceptable, but always with proper form: bend the knees, keep back straight, lift with legs.
41 lbs and up
Group lift only, or use a server lift. No exceptions. Most modern servers and switches need this.
Pre lift check
Clear path, stable footing, communicate with your partner before counting down.

Working at Height

Ladder safety
Three points of contact at all times. Two hands and one foot, or two feet and one hand.
Top of rack
Use a stable platform ladder, not a chair, not a milk crate. Always face the ladder.
Tool drop
Tether tools or use a tool belt. A wrench dropped from rack-top is dangerous and damages gear below.

Electrical and ESD

Power off when possible
Confirm with a meter, do not assume. Lockout tagout if working on circuits.
ESD strap
Wear it whenever handling exposed boards, RAM, GPUs. Static can kill silicon instantly.
No metal jewelry
Watches, rings, bracelets come off near live equipment. Conductors plus current equals injury.

If something goes wrong

Stop work
If a situation feels unsafe, stop. Amazon culture explicitly supports calling a stop.
Report fast
Tell your lead and document immediately. Safety incidents that get reported quickly are easier to fix and less damaging to your record.
Learn from it
Amazon respects people who own mistakes and explain what they will change.

Speaking with Confidence

Sound calm and clear

English does not have to be perfect. What matters is structure, pace, and being understood. These tips are practical and proven.

The 3 second rule

When asked a question, take a deliberate 3 second pause before answering. This is not awkward. It is professional. It tells the interviewer you are thinking, and it gives you time to choose the right opening sentence.

Memorize 5 opening phrases

If your first sentence is solid, the rest flows. Pick a few of these and rehearse until they feel natural.

  • "Let me give you a specific example from my time at..."
  • "Yes, I have actually run into something like this. Here is what happened..."
  • "Good question. The most relevant example I can share is..."
  • "At my current role, I dealt with this kind of situation when..."
  • "There was a time on the job when..."

Phrases for buying time

If you need a moment to think mid answer, these are clean and professional ways to pause.

  • "That is a great question, let me think for a second."
  • "To make sure I answer that correctly, can you give me a moment?"
  • "Just to clarify, are you asking about X or Y?"

If you do not understand a question

Ask. It is a hundred times better than guessing wrong.

  • "Could you rephrase that for me?"
  • "I want to make sure I understand. Are you asking about how I handled the conflict, or how the team responded?"
  • "Sorry, the connection cut for a moment. Can you repeat the question?"

Pace and breathing

Speak slower than you think you need to. Most candidates rush when they get nervous, and rushing makes English harder to follow. Pause between sentences. A small breath between thoughts gives the interviewer time to absorb what you said.

Practice out loud, every day

Pick three of the questions from the Question Bank and answer them out loud, looking at yourself in a mirror or recording on your phone. This is the single most powerful prep technique. Reading silently is not the same.

★ Day of mindset
  • You are not being judged on your accent. They listen for clarity and content.
  • Short complete sentences land better than long winding ones.
  • If you stumble on a word, do not panic. Smile, restart the sentence, keep going.
  • Your real experience is your best ally. Lean on stories you actually lived.
